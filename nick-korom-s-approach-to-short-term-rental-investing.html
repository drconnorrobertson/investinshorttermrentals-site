Nick Korom's Approach to Short-Term Rental Investing

Published May 13, 2026 · Updated September 20, 2026 · By Nick Korom, founder of BNB Accelerator

Short-term rental investing should begin with the investor's portfolio, not a property listing. Direct ownership combines real estate, hospitality operations, local regulation and financing. That combination can be attractive, but only when it fits the investor's capital, time horizon, concentration limits and willingness to own an operating asset.

This is the framework I use when discussing the category through BNB Accelerator. It is general education, not individualized investment, tax or legal advice. Each investor should make decisions with qualified professionals who understand the investor's complete circumstances.

1. Define the job of the investment

An STR can be expected to produce current income, long-term equity, personal use, inflation exposure or potential tax benefits. Those objectives are not interchangeable. A property selected mainly for personal use may not be the strongest cash-flow asset. A property chosen for income may be located somewhere the owner would never vacation.

I prefer to state the objective in plain language before discussing markets: what should this investment contribute, how much capital can be committed, how long can it be held and how involved does the owner want to be? Without that clarity, investors tend to optimize whatever number is most appealing on the listing.

2. Treat direct ownership as a concentrated position

A single property can represent a meaningful percentage of an investor's net worth. It is illiquid, location-specific and exposed to regulation, weather, insurance, financing and local demand. Leverage can magnify both gains and losses.

The relevant question is not whether an STR can produce an attractive return. It is whether the size and risk of the position make sense alongside the investor's business, public-market investments, other real estate and cash reserves. Our STR diversification guide explains why concentration becomes especially important as a portfolio grows.

3. Select a market for resilience, not reputation

Well-known destinations are not automatically strong investments. Popularity can raise acquisition prices, attract new supply and create regulatory pressure. Less famous destinations can offer better economics, but may have thinner demand or fewer capable operators.

I look for understandable travel demand, a regulatory environment that can be verified, sufficient comparable properties, reliable local services and an entry price that leaves room for imperfect performance. The market should be evaluated across the entire year, not only during its strongest season.

4. Use ranges instead of a single return forecast

A forecast should make uncertainty visible. Revenue depends on nightly rate, occupancy, seasonality, property quality, reviews, distribution and management. Expenses depend on insurance, utilities, taxes, maintenance, cleaning, supplies, management and capital replacement.

I would rather see a base case and downside case than one precise projected return. Investors should understand which assumptions drive the result and how much liquidity remains if launch is delayed or revenue is lower. The investor guide to STR revenue projections provides a structured way to review those assumptions.

5. Match leverage to resilience

Debt changes both return potential and fragility. The monthly payment continues when demand slows, a permit is delayed or repairs interrupt availability. Financing should be evaluated on payment, term, amortization, reserves, prepayment provisions and intended use, not interest rate alone.

An investor should know how the property performs under the actual proposed loan and under a reasonable revenue decline. A highly leveraged property that works only in the optimistic case is a different investment from one that can absorb volatility.

6. Reserve capital is part of the investment

The down payment is not the full cash requirement. Closing, repairs, furnishing, design, permits, photography and opening supplies can be substantial. After launch, the property still needs working capital and reserves for maintenance, replacements and interruptions.

Reserves should reflect the property's complexity. Pools, hot tubs, septic systems, older mechanicals and severe-weather exposure can increase the probability of a large expense. Our guide to sizing STR cash reserves separates acquisition cash from operating liquidity.

7. Decide who will operate the asset before buying it

Direct ownership is not automatically passive. Someone must manage pricing, guest communication, cleaning, maintenance, supplies, reviews and emergency response. Even with professional management, the owner remains responsible for overseeing the manager, financial performance and major property decisions.

I want that operating model defined before closing. The availability and quality of local operators can materially affect the investment. Management should be treated as part of the market and property decision, not as a vendor search that begins after the keys are delivered.

8. Keep tax considerations in their proper place

Tax treatment can matter, but it should not convert a weak property into a good one. Eligibility for depreciation, material participation treatment and other outcomes depends on individual facts, documentation and current law. BNB Accelerator is not a CPA firm and does not provide tax advice.

An investor considering tax benefits should involve an independent qualified tax adviser before purchasing. The property should still make sense on its operating and portfolio merits after separating potential tax effects from economic performance.

9. Establish the exit before entry

Every acquisition should include an intended hold period and plausible alternatives. The investor should understand likely resale buyers, transaction costs, financing risk and whether the property has conventional residential appeal outside the STR investor market.

Exit flexibility does not guarantee liquidity, but it improves decision quality. A property that can support several lawful uses generally offers more options than one whose value depends on a single operating model.

What a disciplined STR decision looks like

A disciplined investor can explain why direct ownership belongs in the portfolio, why the position size is reasonable, why the market is resilient, how the forecasts were built, who will operate the property and how the investment can be exited.

BNB Accelerator's role is limited to acquisition and launch coordination. The buyer retains control of the decision and uses independent brokers, lenders, attorneys, CPAs, insurance professionals and property managers where appropriate. Clear boundaries are a feature of a serious process.
