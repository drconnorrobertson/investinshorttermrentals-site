Work from the refund record, not the bank deposit

This original fictional packet concerns ten nights in a converted loft at $400 per night. It is separate from the annual property model above. Assume a documented access interruption leads to a $1,200 accommodation refund and a separate $100 cleaning refund. The packet illustrates how to classify amounts; it does not establish that an actual loft has access problems or that a platform would grant these refunds.

The fictional pre-refund host payout contains $4,000 of room charges and $400 of guest cleaning charges, less a $160 host fee: $4,240. Guest taxes of $300 are assumed collected and remitted outside the host payout. The fee remains $160 after the refund in this packet. Those are explicit teaching assumptions, not a statement of Airbnb’s current fee, tax or refund treatment.

Choose a denominator that matches the model

For this isolated packet, the accommodation-refund fraction is $1,200 ÷ $4,000 = 30%. The worksheet’s refund input applies to accommodation revenue after its separate discount factor; it does not apply to guest cleaning charges or taxes. Do not use ($4,000 − $2,740) ÷ $4,000 = 31.5% as though the deposit difference were entirely an accommodation refund. Do not put the combined $1,300 room-and-cleaning refund over a room-only denominator either.

Thirty percent is this fictional packet’s result, not a recommended annual assumption for the property. Reconcile a representative period, including the original stay and adjustment references, before selecting an annual input. If your revenue records already show refunded room charges net, applying the same refund factor again would deduct them twice. If a cancellation removes nights from your occupancy calculation, identify that treatment separately so a missing stay is not also treated as a second refund loss.

Understand what the existing sensitivity assumes

The annual base illustration above has $75,920 of accommodation revenue. A 2% allowance reduces it by $1,518.40. The model also reduces management, booking fees, routine maintenance and replacement allowances because each is a fraction of the remaining accommodation revenue. Their combined fraction is 32%, so modeled owner cash flow falls by $1,518.40 × 68% = $1,032.51, rounded to cents, while fixed bills and debt remain unchanged.

That is a model assumption, not proof those costs will fall after an actual refund. In CL-01 the $160 fee stays fixed, so a proportional fee reduction would not reproduce the packet. Read the management agreement and transaction record for the actual fee basis, retained charges and timing. Use a separate adjustment outside this model if a material amount cannot be represented faithfully; do not change unrelated inputs simply to force the desired bank total.

Keep the evidence and the open question together

Airbnb’s earnings guidance describes listing/date filters and CSV exports, and its adjustment guidance explains that payout adjustments can arise from cancellations or reservation changes. Those platform references explain available records; the amounts and reconciliation here are original fiction.

  1. Retain a non-sensitive reservation reference, original room-charge amount, separate cleaning and tax lines, and the stay dates.
  2. Match each refund or adjustment to the relevant record and period; distinguish settlement date from the stay being adjusted.
  3. Reconcile the provider’s fee and payout detail to the bank deposit without treating the deposit as room revenue.
  4. Document which losses are already netted from occupancy or revenue before choosing a refund sensitivity.
