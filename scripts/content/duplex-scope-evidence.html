Define one unit or the whole building before using a worksheet

Reviewed November 21, 2026. The three-bedroom label is a hypothetical profile, not an address, floor plan or confirmation of lawful rental capacity. For the original teaching example here, assume a building with two separately rented units: Unit A has two bedrooms and Unit B has one. Three bedrooms therefore describe the building total in this example. Do not silently apply that description to every unit or infer that either proposed use is permitted.

The linked worksheets use a single set of revenue, financing and expense assumptions. Start by writing whether your set concerns Unit A, Unit B, or the whole building. Keep the purchase-price denominator, loan, entry cash, revenue and expenses on that same boundary. A unit-only revenue figure compared with whole-building debt can make an allocation look like a property loss; a building-wide revenue figure compared with only one unit’s costs can overstate cash flow.

A fictional shared-utility packet

Assume all three bills below cover the same twelve months and are supported by the teaching packet: Unit A has $960 of separately metered electricity, Unit B has $1,440, and the building has $2,400 of shared water and common-area service. There are no arrears, owner reimbursements, deposits or other utility items. The full-building utility total is $960 + $1,440 + $2,400 = $4,800. These are fictional review inputs, not local utility quotes or a recommended budget.

The equal split assigns half of the shared $2,400 to each unit. The second illustration assigns 120 ÷ 300 = 40% to A and 180 ÷ 300 = 60% to B. Under either method the two unit totals sum to $4,800. Unit A’s $240 difference is a change in allocation, not a newly discovered bill or a reduction in the building’s total spending. Do not add both methods together.

A booked-night split is only a comparison method here. It does not establish consumption, a contract requirement, a tax allocation or a rule for billing occupants. Shared costs can continue during vacancy. Obtain the meter scope, invoices and relevant agreements before selecting a method, and retain the reason for the allocation. Use unit-nights for separate calendars

In the same fictional year, assume both units were available for 365 nights. A has 120 ÷ 365, or about 32.9%, paid occupancy; B has 180 ÷ 365, or about 49.3%. The pooled figure is 300 booked unit-nights divided by 730 available unit-nights, about 41.1%. The count of calendar dates when at least one unit was booked is not supplied: overlapping stays matter, so you cannot derive it from those totals alone.

The linked calculator uses one 365-night available calendar before owner blocks. It does not model two separate unit calendars. Use a consistent single-unit or whole-building rentable-product scope that the inputs actually represent; do not insert combined unit-nights into its one-calendar occupancy field. If you need independent calendars, separate unit results first and reconcile the building totals outside this model. Two units can together exceed 365 booked unit-nights without either unit exceeding its own available calendar.

Keep financing, retained cash and permissions separate

A shared loan still has to be paid even if one unit is vacant. Allocating part of that loan payment to a unit is a reporting convention; it does not change the lender’s claim or demonstrate that either unit can support its own financing. For a whole-building cash review, include the full debt payment once. For a unit analysis, show the assigned share and the bridge back to the full obligation.

Retained reserves, furnishing commitments and shared repairs also need a scope label. Assign a refrigerator to the unit it serves, document a common-system repair separately, and avoid counting one retained reserve account as independently available to both units. None of this arithmetic establishes legal use, room capacity, insurance coverage or the condition of a shared system. Keep those evidence questions open until the appropriate records and responses address them.
